\section{Generators}

\noindent The rest of this document concerns the relationships between Lie
groups and Lie algebras. The first of these relationships comes via 
\firstmention{generators}.

\begin{definition}[Generator]
    A generator $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ is a Lie-algebra element that, multiplied by
    a real $\theta$,\sidenote{$X$ is a vector in the Lie Algebra $\mathfrak{g}$
        as a vector space, and vectors can always be multiplied by scalars from
        the underlying field of the vector space, by vector-space rules}
    produces a an element of a Lie group G under the exponential map:
    \begin{align*}
        \exp &: (\mathfrak{g} \times \mathbb{R}) \rightarrow G \\
        h(\theta) &= \exp(\theta{}X)
    \end{align*}
\end{definition}

The exponential map is abstract, here. It must be further defined for any
concretization. Physicists work almost exclusively with matrix concretizations
for the sake of calculation. In such concretizations, $\exp$ is a matrix
exponential, defined by Taylor series. From this point on, assume all
concretizations are matrix concretizations. We hope it's obvious from context
when we're working abstractly and when we're working concretely.

\begin{remark} A generator $X\in\su{2}$ in the Lie algebra can be recovered 
    from a Lie-group element $h(\theta)$ as follows:
    \begin{equation*}
        X = \left.\frac{d h(\theta)}{d \theta}\right|_{\theta=0}
    \end{equation*}
\end{remark}


Many Lie groups can map to the same Lie algebra. 

\begin{example}
    \SO{3} and \SU{2} have the same Lie algebra: \su{2}. Begin by
    considering \SO{3}\sidenote{\SO{3} is also a Lie group, of course} as a
    matrix group: the set of real, $3\times{}3$ orthogonal matrices with
    determinant 1. 

\begin{proposition}
    Real $3\times{}3$ generators $J\in\su{2}$ of the special orthogonal group
    \SO{3} are traceless and antisymmetric.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\leavevmode
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep] % Removes space between items
    \item \textit{Traceless}:
    ``Special'' means that the elements $O\in\SO{3}$ have unit determinant.
    That fact implies that the generators are traceless:\sidenote{Equation
    numbers with prefixes, such as (Sch:3.60), are taken from other sources
    that are meant to be obvious from the prefix.}
        \begin{equation}
            \det{O}\mustbe{1}\quad\implies\quad\det{e^{\theta{}J}}=e^{\theta\;\trace{J}}\mustbe{}1
            \tag{Sch:3.60}
        \end{equation}
    \item \textit{Antisymmetric}: 
        ``Orthogonal'' means that elements $O$ of \SO{3} satisfy 
        $\transpose{O}O=O\transpose{O}\mustbe{1}$. Therefore:
        \begin{equation}
            {\transpose{O}}O=e^{\theta\transpose{J}}
                               e^{\theta{}J}\mustbe{}1
                       \quad\implies\quad
                       \transpose{J}\mustbe{}-J
        \tag{Sch:3.59}
    \end{equation}
    The \textit{transpose} operation lifts into the exponent, as can be see by
    a Taylor expansion, because $\theta$ is a scalar.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\noindent Both \su{2} and \SO(3) are three-dimensional, \su{2} in the sense
that a basis requires three linearly independent generators $\{X_1$, $X_2$,
$X_3\}$ to span the space, \SO{3} in the sense that its matrix form consists of
$3\times{}3$ matrices. A basis for the following \textit{adjoint
representation}\sidenote{This usage of ``adjoint'' is distinct from the adjoint
operation on matrices and is explained in the next section.}\sidenote{Informally,
    for now,
take ``representation'' to mean ``matrices corresponding to elements of a Lie
group or Lie algebra.'' We'll make this notion rigorous shortly.} of \su{2} is

{
\small
\addtolength{\arraycolsep}{-3pt}
\begin{equation}
    J_1 = 
    \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & -1 \\
        0 & 1 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}, \quad
    J_2 = 
    \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & 0 & 1 \\
        0 & 0 & 0 \\
        -1 & 0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}, \quad
    J_3 = 
    \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & -1 & 0 \\
        1 & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}
    \tag{Sch:3.61}
\end{equation}
}

\noindent We note, in passing, that the Lie bracket can ``generate'' the basis
(in a different sense of the word \textit{generate}), from just two of the $J$
vectors / matrices,\sidenote{``Vectors'' in \su{2} are matrices in this
representation.} because $[J_1, J_2]=J_3$, etc.

The following Mathematica code checks that these generators in \su{2} satisfy
the Lie-algebra commutation relations $[J_i, J_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}
J_k$:\sidenote{$\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the totally antisymmetric Levi-Civita symbol
    that equals $1$ when $ijk$ is an even permutation of $123$, $-1$ when
${ijk}$ is an odd permutation, and $0$ otherwise. This expression is
\textit{not} using the Einstein summation convention; it's just ordinary
scalar-vector (as matrix) multiplication for any $i$, $j$, and $k$.}

\begin{Verbatim}[frame=single, label=Mathematica]
J1 = {{0, 0, 0}, {0, 0, -1}, {0, 1, 0}};
J2 = {{0, 0, 1}, {0, 0, 0}, {-1, 0, 0}};
J3 = {{0, -1, 0}, {1, 0, 0}, {0, 0, 0}};

J1.J2 - J2.J1 == J3 (* True *)
J2.J3 - J3.J2 == J1 (* True *)
J3.J1 - J1.J3 == J2 (* True *)
\end{Verbatim}

\noindent Mathematica can further check that the matrix exponentials of these
generators span \SO{3} as a matrix group. The following should suffice to
convince you of this fact, assuming that you are familiar with elementary
rotation matrices:

\begin{Verbatim}[frame=single, label=Mathematica]
MatrixExp[\[Theta] J1]
{{1, 0, 0}, 
 {0, Cos[\[Theta]], -Sin[\[Theta]]}, 
 {0, Sin[\[Theta]],  Cos[\[Theta]]}}
\end{Verbatim}

\begin{proposition}
    Complex $2\times{}2$ generators $T\in\su{2}$ of the special unitary group \SU{2} 
    are traceless and anti-Hermitian.\sidenote{``Anti-Hermitian'' for complex matrices
        is analogous to antisymmetric for real matrices like the adjoint representation
    of \SO{3} above.}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}

    Proof of the traceless property goes just as above. 

    ``Unitary'' means that the elements $U$ of \SU{2} satisfy
    $\adjoint{U}U=U\adjoint{U}\mustbe{1}$, where $\adjoint{U}$ is complex
    conjugate transpose, or \textit{adjoint}, symbolized by the $\dagger$. \sidenote{
        As noted above, this meaning of ``adjoint'' is distinct from its meaning 
        in ``adjoint representation'', which pertains to the representation of \SO{3} 
        above.}

    ``Hermitian'' means that a matrix is self-adjoint. ``Anti-Hermitian'' means
    that a matrix equals the negative of its adjoint. Now we see that
        \begin{equation}
            {\adjoint{U}}{U}=e^{\theta\adjoint{T}}
                             e^{\theta{}T}\mustbe{}1
                       \quad\implies\quad
                       \adjoint{T}\mustbe{}-T
        \end{equation}

\noindent The adjoint operation also lifts into the exponent, like
\textit{transpose}, because $\theta$ is a \textit{real} scalar. 

\end{proof}

\noindent A basis for this \textit{fundamental
representation}\sidenote{Clarified below.} is $-i/2$ times the three
\textit{Pauli matrices}:

{
\small
\addtolength{\arraycolsep}{-3pt}
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
    \sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
    \sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.
    \tag{Sch:3.80}
\end{equation}
}

\noindent The basis is $T_1 = -i\sigma_1/2$, $T_2 = -i\sigma_2/2$, $T_3 = -i
\sigma_3/2$ as can be readily verified in Mathematica, showing, again in
passing, that the factor of $1/2$ maintains the unit \textit{structure
constants} $\epsilon_{ijk}$:\sidenote{We'll see more diverse structure constants
in the sequel.} Without the $1/2$, we'd be chasing around factors of $2$. 
This fact is related to the \textit{double covering} of the fundamental representation
over the adjoint representation, a notion we'll get back to.

\begin{Verbatim}[frame=single, label=Mathematica]
T1 = -I/2 PauliMatrix[1];
T2 = -I/2 PauliMatrix[2];
T3 = -I/2 PauliMatrix[3];
T1 . T2 - T2 . T1 == T3 (* True *)
T2 . T3 - T3 . T2 == T1 (* True *)
T3 . T1 - T1 . T3 == T2 (* True *)
\end{Verbatim}

\end{example}
